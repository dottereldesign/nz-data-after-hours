Favourites

1. They Aired That? — NZ’s messiest broadcasts

Every case opens with a headline. You get the setup, pick “crossed the line” or “fair game,” then see what happened, how other readers voted, and what the regulator decided. The homepage chart ranks cases by where readers disagree most with the ruling. That gives people a reason to send one to the group chat: “You think that was fine? Vote before you read the ending.”

There’s enough material for very different episodes. In another case, a radio host posed as a police officer and told a husband his wife was seeking a restraining order against his mother. The husband said he wanted a divorce and hung up. The BSA criticised the station’s consent process but ultimately accepted that consent to broadcast the recording had been obtained. That detail is worth a second vote: does agreeing to air it afterward change your answer? The source is the BSA’s public decisions archive, where each case has to be read and tagged. 2. Someone Tried “Kyng” — the baby-name spelling wars

This one is a downloadable CSV in your original catalogue. From 2009–2024, the Registrar-General recorded 76 declines for King, 4 for Kyng and 2 for Kingz. Prince has an even better family tree: 71 for Prince, 8 for Prynce and 3 for Prinz. Then there’s the person who submitted Suprintedent. That spelling is in the file. Declined names dataset and CSV

3. The R18 T-Shirt — ordinary objects that ended up before NZ’s censor

A member of the public submitted a Cradle of Filth T-shirt for classification. It was initially banned and later rated R18. An illustrated soft-drink can also went through classification and came out unrestricted. The office has even classified jigsaws, bumper stickers and campervans. Official classification timeline · What the office classifies

I’d build this as a shop window. Click a shirt, a can or a book. Guess the rating. Then slide between the original decision and the later one wherever there was a review. Put the T-shirt next to Fifty Shades of Grey, which was classified unrestricted. “Which one got the adults-only rating?” is a homepage people can play before they’ve even decided whether they care about censorship. The source is the Classification Office’s own register. Official decisions register · Both classifications

4. Who Got the Pokie Money? — the one your town would argue about

NZ publishes approved and declined pokie-grant applications, down to the organisation and amount. In the actual file, Pub Charity Limited approved $4,500 for Manaia School in Taranaki for kapa haka. Twelve days later, it declined $1,743.99 for the Thames Museum Society. The records give the outcomes and amounts. The reason for each decision is a separate question. Class 4 grants dataset

The homepage is one search box: “Did your club get pokie money?” Type a school, rugby club or town and see every application, how much was requested, how much came through, and who else applied to that society that year. Then add the uncomfortable local chart: how much players lost on pokies in that district, alongside how much grant funding was recorded for it. Keep multi-district grants visible so readers can see where the money trail gets fuzzy. This is the one I’d expect to spread through local Facebook groups. District gambling expenditure data
